## HARMFUL — wrong chemistry a student would copy into an exam

Every boxed answer, every reaction condition and every named product in this group is correct as printed.
The re-derivations that matter: blister copper = converter product ≈98 % Cu, blisters from dissolved SO₂
escaping on freezing, `Cu₂S + 2Cu₂O → 6Cu + SO₂`; NaCN depresses ZnS so PbS floats, via
`4NaCN + ZnS → Na₂[Zn(CN)₄] + Na₂S` (NCERT's exact form, and the 2− charge on [Zn(CN)₄]²⁻ confirmed);
roasting = air / sulphides, calcination = no air / carbonates + hydrated oxides, both below the melting
point; zone refining (impurity more soluble in the melt, one-direction passes, Si/Ge/Ga), electrolytic
refining (impure anode, anode mud), poling (green wood, own-oxide impurity, Cu and Sn), vapour-phase
refining (Mond, van Arkel); zinc: froth flotation → `2ZnS + 3O₂ → 2ZnO + 2SO₂` (1200 K) →
`ZnO + C → Zn + CO` (1673 K, zinc distils since it boils at 1180 K) → electrolytic refining in acidified
ZnSO₄; copper: `2CuFeS₂ + O₂ → Cu₂S + 2FeS + SO₂`, `FeO + SiO₂ → FeSiO₃` slag, matte = Cu₂S + FeS,
self-reduction in the converter; Bayer's process including the silica equation
`SiO₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂SiO₃ + H₂O`; Hall–Héroult with `Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al`, `C + O²⁻ → CO + 2e⁻`,
`C + 2O²⁻ → CO₂ + 4e⁻` and overall `2Al₂O₃ + 3C → 4Al + 3CO₂` (all charge-balanced).

## Teacher-gate questions

1. **Pine oil — collector or frothing agent?** `ts_ipe_c2_met_froth_flotation` states flatly (in s4 and in the
   `insider_note`, which the chatbot speaks first) that "pine oil is the frothing agent". NCERT Class 12
   §6.2 lists "pine oils, fatty acids, xanthates" together as **collectors**. Mineral-processing practice
   and most Indian board keys treat pine oil as the frother. The card's other correction — sodium ethyl
   xanthate is a collector, not a frothing agent — is unambiguous and right; only the pine-oil half is a
   convention split. Which does the TS key accept, and should the insider note hedge?
2. **Copper smelting furnace.** The cards put roasting in a reverberatory furnace and smelting in a **blast
   furnace** (and `..._blister_copper` s1 `common_mistakes[1]` teaches "the blast furnace gives matte").
   NCERT describes the sulphide ore heated with silica in a **reverberatory furnace** to give matte and never
   mentions a blast furnace for copper. Both appear in Indian texts; confirm the board's wording.
3. **The Bayer precipitation equation.** The card prints the balanced `2Na[Al(OH)₄] + 2CO₂ → Al₂O₃·3H₂O +
   2NaHCO₃` and lists the NCERT/source form (one CO₂, two NaHCO₃) as a student mistake. The NCERT form is
   genuinely unbalanced (verified), but if the board's key reproduces NCERT verbatim, confirm a balanced
   answer is not marked down — and that Al₂O₃·3H₂O is accepted where the key writes Al(OH)₃.
4. **NaAlO₂ vs Na[Al(OH)₄]** as the leaching product (see the WRONG row above) — which form does the TS key
   expect, and is the older meta-aluminate form still accepted?
5. **The mark splits are the bank's claim, not the board's.** All nine `verification_note`s say so, and they
   also record that no two-book union check and no board back-test were possible for Chemistry-II (the BLM on
   hand is physics-only and first-year; no second-year chemistry paper is in the corpus). Every 1+1 and
   1+1+1+1 split in this group needs a Telangana IPE teacher's confirmation, as does the decision in
   `ts_ipe_c2_met_froth_flotation` that the flotation-cell diagram earns **0** marks because the question says
   "explain", not "draw".
6. **Iron pyrites FeS₂** is offered as a froth-flotation example (`ts_ipe_c2_met_froth_flotation` s5). It is a
   sulphide and it does float, but iron is not extracted from it — is it in the key's example list?
